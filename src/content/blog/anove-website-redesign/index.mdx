## Introduction

I started at Anove writing how-to articles and grinding Excel sheets. Now I
somehow ship production websites. The recent culmination of this has been the
[company website](https://www.anove.ai) redesign.

I built this website from scratch over the past two years. It started when we
ditched our low-code setup for something we actually owned. Back then we were a
tiny startup figuring things out; now we're trying to look like a real company.
I matured too—learning to actually _own_ something instead of just writing code
someone else spec'd out or making drag-and-dropping components. What I thought
would be a quick color refresh turned into six months of learning how business
actually works. These are the mistakes I made and what I'd change if I did it
again.

## Starting out slow

It started out with a simple goal, at least in theory: to make the website more
modern and marketing-oriented. This meant: adjusting the layout and making some
new CTA components, which didn't sound like too big of a task. To achieve this,
I worked closely with the marketing professional managing our social media
accounts, because her expertise was something really valuable in such a task.

### Gathering information

We started out by getting a lay of the land. We analyzed competitor websites to
see how they do it. _How is their header organized?_ _How often do they use
CTAs?_ From this we mapped out the basic layout of the pages we wanted to adjust
and add, and then I got to work.

To analyze my work, I needed feedback, because your own eyes are often clouded
and biased when you're deeply involved in such a project. I started by asking
the marketing professional, but also other positions at the company, for
"general" feedback on the website. Then I'd go back for another round, implement
more changes, and repeat the cycle.

The feedback I was getting was conflicting, leading me down confusing paths and
wasting time. One person thought that a screenshot was beautiful and perfectly
placed, while the other thought it was distracting, and this is just _one_ of
the many examples. This approach clearly wasn't working.

I started doing some research as to why, and it turned out "general" feedback is
not enough. I needed specific, actionable input from not only colleagues, but
also the target audience and stakeholders who would actually use the product. I
realized that without targeting specific questions to specific groups of people,
I would just be building for their _opinion_, and we all know how subjective
people can be. I think this is true for any kind of product, not just websites.
This new approach of gathering feedback was more effective, and it showed in the
progress that was being made.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Example questions">
    To colleagues, you ask concrete, objective questions:
    1. Does the visual style match the brand guideline?
    2. Does anything look 'broken' or 'bugged'?

    To the target audience, or even random people, you ask questions to see if they understand the key value proposition quickly:
    1. Set a timer for exactly 10 seconds, open the website and look around. Once the timer is up, close the website. What problem do you think we solve, and why would it benefit you/someone else?

    To designers and marketing you actually can, and honestly _must_, ask subjective questions:
    1. Do you think this CTA is placed correctly?
    2. Does the page have a clear visual hierarchy?
    3. Is this component visually striking?

</Callout>
